The state of the ocean

The oceans were at the origin of life.

They still provide the basic conditions for life on Earth: oceans regulate our climate, support countless species, sustain our food, and supports millions of jobs. Yet they are under increasing pressure from pollution, overuse, and climate change.

The UN Ocean Decade (2021–2030) is a global effort to turn scientific knowledge into action:

better monitoring, smarter management, and concrete solutions for a healthy ocean. It brings together governments, scientists, and companies around one shared goal: protect, and restore our oceans before it is too late.

By studying fish scientists can see the oceans changing:

arming waters, shifting currents, pollution, and disappearing habitats. This knowledge helps us understand where marine life is thriving, and where it is under pressure.

Five oceans, four problems:

Mineral extraction can disturb habitats that formed over thousands of years and are still barely understood. Many deep-sea species live only in very specific conditions and recover extremely slowly. Large machines scraping the seabed destroy these fragile ecosystems, create sediment plumes that spread over long distances, and generate noise and light in otherwise dark, quiet environments. Deep-sea ecosystems also play a role in the Earth’s climate system. The ocean floor helps lock away carbon for very long periods of time. Disturbing these sediments and habitats may release stored carbon and interfere with natural processes that move carbon from the surface to the deep ocean. Although the full climate impact of deep sea mining is still uncertain, there is growing concern that it could weaken the ocean’s ability to buffer climate change.

Overfishing is catching fish faster than they can reproduce, driving fish stocks toward collapse and degrading marine environments. It threatens global food security and drives the loss of ocean biodiversity. When key species disappear or decline, food webs become unbalanced, habitats such as coral reefs and seagrass beds deteriorate, and coastal communities lose important sources of food and income.

Every year, it is estimated a million tonnes of plastic waste leaks into our oceans. Once in the ocean, plastic breaks down into smaller and smaller pieces, but it does not disappear. It can injure or kill marine animals, damage habitats such as coral reefs and beaches, and carry toxic substances through the food web including into seafood we eat. Plastic pollution can also alter natural processes, reducing ecosystems’ ability to adapt to climate change. For example, plastics smother coastal vegetation like seagrass and mangroves that store large amounts of carbon, and they stress organisms that build reefs and shells.

Bottom trawling is a fishing method in which heavy nets are dragged across the seafloor to catch species like shrimp, flatfish, and other bottom-dwelling fish. Coral reefs, sponges, and seabed ecosystems can be reduced to rubble after a single trawl. Once gone, many of these deep habitats may take centuries to recover. These seabed communities are home to a great diversity of life and play an important role in storing carbon in seafloor sediments. Repeated trawling disturbs these sediments, resuspends carbon, and can weaken the ocean’s ability to act as a long-term carbon sink
